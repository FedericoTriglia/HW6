Statistical Independence

Build a contingency table for two categorical variables, inspect marginal and conditional frequencies, and perform Pearson's chi-square test of independence.

1. Enter a bivariate dataset

Paste one observation per line, with exactly two columns. The first column is variable X and the second is variable F. Commas, semicolons, and tabs are supported; quoted values are allowed.

The example concerns study method and exam outcome. It contains 120 observations and is loaded automatically.

2. Results

Marginal frequencies

The marginal frequency of each category is its total count divided by N.

Observed contingency table

Each cell contains an observed count. The final column and row show the row and column totals.

Conditional frequencies: P(F | X)

Within each row, percentages sum to 100% (up to rounding).

Conditional frequencies: P(X | F)

Within each column, percentages sum to 100% (up to rounding).

Expected frequencies under H₀: independence

The Pearson contribution of each cell is (O − E)² / E. The test statistic is the sum of all cell contributions.

Interpretation and limitations

A statistically significant result indicates evidence of association, not proof that one variable causes the other. The chi-square test detects departures from independence but does not identify a causal mechanism or the direction of an effect.

3. Statistical method

For observed count Oij, row total ni·, column total n·j, and sample size N, the expected count under independence is:

Eij = (ni· × n·j) / N

The Pearson chi-square statistic and its degrees of freedom are:

χ² = Σi Σj (Oij − Eij)² / Eij,   df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

The null hypothesis states that X and F are independent in the population. The alternative states that they are associated. The p-value is the upper-tail probability P(Χ²df ≥ χ²observed) under the null hypothesis.
